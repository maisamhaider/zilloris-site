Privacy Policy

App: Arkiv (com.zilloris.arkiv)
Updated: 9 November 2026
Contact: support@zilloris.com

The short version

Your family's papers stay on your phone. The papers, their pictures, and the names, numbers and dates in them are kept on your device, encrypted, and are never uploaded by the app. The one exception is a PDF you make to share or print: it is not encrypted, and it waits in Arkiv's private storage for about an hour, until Arkiv deletes it (see Sharing and printing). There is no Arkiv account and no Arkiv server.

The app sends five kinds of technical data to Google. Three go only if you say yes: crash reports, counts of how the app is used, and speed timings. Two go whatever you answer: requests for the app's settings, and Google ML Kit's short notes on how each scan and each reading of a page went. None of them ever carries a paper, a picture, a name, a number or a date. There is no advertising and no advertising identifier, and nothing is sold or used to profile you.

Your papers only leave the phone when you send them yourself: when you share or print one, keep a backup in a place you chose (such as your own Google Drive), save pages as pictures into your gallery, or put reminder dates in a calendar that syncs. Opening a backup also keeps that same backup file up to date (see Backups).

What the app stores, and where

The people you add (a name and a colour for each drawer), your documents (their kind, name, number, issue and end dates, and the day you added them), the pictures of their pages, a list of the calendar entries Arkiv made (so it can remove them), your backup password and place if you keep a backup, and your settings. All of it is kept in the app's own private storage on your phone.

The list of documents is kept in an encrypted database, and every picture is encrypted too. Their keys are locked by your phone's own key store, which on most phones is built into its secure hardware. Another app on the phone cannot read them.

Google's parts of the app keep small files of their own here too: random identifiers for this installation, the settings the app downloads from Google, and notes waiting to be sent (ML Kit's notes and, if you said yes, usage counts and speed timings). A crash report waits here until it is sent; if your answer was no, it is deleted the next time the app starts. Arkiv does not encrypt these files, but none of them holds anything from your papers.

Android's own phone backup does not copy Arkiv's data, and neither does moving to a new phone through Android's transfer. The only backups of your papers are the ones you make, described below.

What leaves the device

What you send yourself: a paper you share or print, a PDF you save, pages you save as pictures, the backup you keep, and reminder dates you put in your calendar, all described below.

And Google: the settings requests and ML Kit's notes on each scan and reading go whatever you answer. Crash reports, usage counts and speed timings go only if you say yes. Google Play also checks for a newer version of the app. Your papers, their pictures, and the names, numbers and dates in them are never sent by the app.

Crash reports, usage counts, speed timings, and settings

Crash reports, usage counts and speed timings are optional. Once you have a paper in, the app asks you on a later visit, or as soon as you bring your papers back from a backup. If you leave the app without answering, it asks again next time. Closing the question counts as no, and then it is not asked again. None of these three is sent unless you say yes, and you can change your answer at any time in Settings → Help make Arkiv better. The app works exactly the same either way.

Saying no, then or later, deletes the crash reports the app is holding back and resets the usage-count data kept on the phone; a crash report from a time your answer was no is never sent. Something already handed to Google's uploader while your answer was yes (for example, waiting until the phone is back online) can still be sent.

Crash reports (Firebase Crashlytics). If the app crashes or freezes, a report is sent so the fault can be fixed: what the code was doing, the app version, your phone's make, model and processor, its Android version and whether it is rooted, how much memory and storage it has and how much is in use, the battery level and whether it is charging, which way the screen was turned, your language and country setting, time zone, network type and mobile network, and two random installation identifiers. While this is on, Firebase also gets a short note each time the app opens, with the installation identifier, your phone model and Android version, so it can count how often the app runs without crashing. Arkiv puts nothing of yours in either: no paper and nothing you typed.

Usage counts (Firebase Analytics). Firebase counts how often and how long the app is used, when it was first opened, which of its screens open, when it crashes, when its data is cleared, when the app is uninstalled, and app and Android updates. With these go your Android and app version, language, time zone, the store that installed the app, and your country and region (worked out from your network address). These are kept under a random installation identifier, not your name or email. Arkiv adds no counts of its own: nothing about which papers you keep. The advertising identifier is switched off in the app itself, and so is any use of these counts for ads: there are no ads, so there is nothing to measure.

Speed timings (Firebase Performance Monitoring). The app measures how long it takes to start, how smoothly its screens draw, how much memory and processor it uses, and how long Google's own requests inside the app take. These go with a random installation identifier, the app version, your phone model, Android version, language, network type and mobile network. Never content.

Settings (Firebase Remote Config). Each time the app starts, at most once every 12 hours, it asks Google for its own settings: the address of this policy and of the terms, and whether to offer, or sometimes require, an update. The app also starts by itself, without you opening it: each morning to check your reminders, and after the phone restarts, the app is updated, or the clock or time zone changes. So this request can go out on a day you do not open Arkiv.

To ask, it sends a random installation identifier and the usual technical details: app version, Android version, phone make and model, language, country setting, time zone, the store that installed the app, the days the app ran, and your network address. The app runs each morning to check your reminders, so that list of days usually covers every day, not only the days you opened it. If usage counts are on, it also sends when the app was first opened. The request also names the app itself (its package name, its signing fingerprint and the versions of Google's code inside it), which is the same for every copy of Arkiv. Google's speed-timing part inside the app may also ask Google for its own settings in the same way, at most every 12 hours, even if you said no to speed timings; it sends the same details. After a yes, crash reports and their session notes (Firebase Crashlytics and Sessions) fetch their own settings from Google in the same way. No paper travels with any of these. They happen whether or not you said yes to the three above, because they are how the app learns a new address for this policy or the terms, and whether to offer or require an update, without a new version of the app.

These services are run for us by Google LLC, which processes this data on our behalf under Google's privacy policy. The detailed records behind usage counts are kept for 2 months and crash reports for 90 days, then Google deletes them. Totals that no longer point to any installation can stay in the reports. Speed timings and ML Kit's notes are kept on Google's own schedule. There is no account, so none of it can be looked up by who you are.

Scanning a paper

When you scan a paper, Google's document scanner opens. It is part of Google Play services on your phone: it finds the paper's edges, straightens and cleans the page, and hands the pages straight back to Arkiv, which encrypts them and deletes the plain copies the scanner handed over. Google Play services may download the scanner the first time it is used. That screen belongs to Google Play services, not to Arkiv; Google explains what it does with it in Google's privacy policy.

Arkiv then reads the dates, the document number and, from the machine lines of a passport or ID card, the holder's name. It does this on your phone, with Google's ML Kit and a reading model built into the app. Reading a page sends nothing anywhere. The pictures and what Arkiv read only leave the phone when you share, print, copy or back up a paper, or save its pages to your gallery, as described below.

Whatever you answered above, ML Kit sends Google short notes each time a paper is scanned or read: which scanner or reader ran and with what settings, how many pages, how long it took, the picture's size and format, whether any text was found, and any error. With them go the app's name and version, your phone's make, model and build, its Android version, its languages, country setting and time zone, your mobile network and connection type, and your network address. Some of these notes go through Google Play services on your phone, and they can wait on the phone and be sent later, even while Arkiv is closed. They also carry a random ML Kit identifier for this installation; saying no does not reset it. It goes when you uninstall Arkiv or clear its storage. The notes never carry the text that was read or the picture.

On a phone without Google's scanner, Arkiv uses the camera itself to add a new paper, and reads it on the phone in the same way. A photo you choose from your gallery is read the same way too; Arkiv keeps its own encrypted copy, and the photo in your gallery is left as it was. "Scan again" on a paper you already have replaces its pages, kept encrypted, but Arkiv does not read them; on a phone without Google's scanner it opens your gallery instead.

Reminders and your calendar

Reminders before a paper runs out are scheduled and shown by your phone. No message comes from a server. Arkiv's own reminder says whose paper it is and what kind, for example "Sara's passport — 30 days left". Your phone may show it on the lock screen unless you choose to hide notification content there in your phone's settings.

If you switch on "Also put them in my calendar" in Settings, Arkiv writes all-day events titled "Renew a paper — open Arkiv" on the days your reminders fall, for every paper, not only one. Two other buttons switch on the same option: "Add to calendar" on a paper, and "Turn it on" next to the calendar in "Make sure reminders arrive". The events go into one calendar on your phone: the main calendar of one of your accounts, or, if there is none, the first calendar Arkiv is allowed to add to. If you have more than one account on the phone (a work account, for example), you cannot choose which one Arkiv uses. The events carry no name and no kind of paper, so the calendar and its sync never show which paper it is. If that calendar is synced — a Google calendar, for example — its provider stores those events like any others.

Switching the option off removes the dates Arkiv put in your calendar. Arkiv asks for calendar access again if it needs it to do this; if you refuse, the dates stay and the option stays on. If Arkiv has no calendar access when you delete a paper or a drawer, or open a backup, it keeps a note of those dates and takes them out the next time it runs with access.

Sharing and printing

"Share it as a PDF", "Print" and the PDF tools make a file in Arkiv's private storage, where no other app can reach it, and hand it to the app or printer you pick. What happens next is up to you and that app. Arkiv deletes each of these files about an hour after it was made, though a resting phone may do it later; it also deletes any that are more than an hour old each time it opens or closes. In the PDF tools, "Save it on the phone" also keeps a copy in the place you pick, such as your Downloads folder or your own Google Drive. Arkiv does not delete that copy; it stays there until you delete it.

"PDF into pictures" is different: it saves each page as an ordinary picture in your gallery, in Pictures/Arkiv. These pictures are not locked. Your gallery app, its cloud backup if you use one, and any other app you have let see your photos can see them. They stay there until you delete them, even if you uninstall Arkiv.

If you tap Copy next to a date or a number on a paper, Arkiv puts it on your phone's clipboard marked as sensitive, so Android 13 and later hide it from the preview. If you select text in a box yourself and copy it while adding or changing a paper, it is not marked, and Android may show it.

Backups

"Save a backup file" makes one file, locked with a password you choose, and saves it where you pick.

"Keep a backup up to date" asks for a password and then a place, chosen with your phone's own file picker — usually your Google Drive. Arkiv locks the backup with your password on your phone and writes it to that place again shortly after every change. The app that owns the place (Google Drive, for example) stores the file and carries it, under your own account with that app and its terms. Google Drive may also keep older versions of it; an older version can still hold papers you have since deleted from Arkiv, and it opens with the same password. Arkiv never uploads it itself. The password and the place are kept on your phone, encrypted. Turning the switch off stops the renewing; the file stays where it is until you delete it.

"Open a backup" switches this on too, if the place lets Arkiv change the file. Once your papers are back, Arkiv keeps writing your changes to the file you opened, locked with the password you typed to open it, instead of any place you chose before. Settings then shows "Keep a backup up to date" as on; switch it off if you only wanted to open the file.

A backup password has at least 10 characters. Without it the file cannot be opened — not by us, not by Google, and not by you. That only holds if the password is hard to guess: anyone who has the file can try as many passwords as they like, so use a few words, not 1234567890. Write it down somewhere that is not this phone. We cannot recover a lost password.

Permissions

Camera: to scan a paper on phones without Google's scanner. Notifications: for the reminders, if you allow them. Calendar: only if you switch on the calendar option (in Settings or on a paper). Arkiv reads the list of your calendars, but only each one's number, whether Arkiv may add to it, and whether it is an account's main calendar, to pick one to add to (see Reminders and your calendar). Arkiv never reads your events; it adds and removes only its own.

Lock: when Arkiv opens, and again when you come back to it after more than a minute away, your phone asks for your fingerprint, your face, or your screen lock (PIN, pattern or password). It does not ask when you come back within 10 minutes from a screen Arkiv opened for you, such as the scanner, the share or print screen, the file picker or Google Play's update screen. This is on by default when your phone has a screen lock, and you can switch it off in Settings. Your phone checks them; Arkiv never sees them. On Android 9 and older, storage: to save pictures from a PDF into your gallery.

The app also holds Android's two ordinary network permissions: one to use the internet, and one to see whether your phone is online and which kind of network it uses. They are used for the settings requests and ML Kit's notes, and only if you say yes, for crash reports, usage counts and speed timings. To see whether a newer version exists, Arkiv asks the Google Play app on your phone, and Google Play checks by itself; that check carries nothing about your papers. The advertising-ID and install-referrer permissions that analytics libraries usually bring along have been removed from this app.

Arkiv also holds a few ordinary permissions that Android grants without asking. One lets Arkiv start after the phone restarts, so it can book the morning check again and send any reminder that came due while the phone was off; Arkiv also starts after an update and when the clock or time zone changes, for the same reason. One keeps the phone awake for a moment while a background task finishes: the morning check, renewing your backup or clearing old PDFs. One is for long background tasks, which Arkiv does not use. Android's scheduling library brings these; Google's libraries also ask for the stay-awake one. On some phones Arkiv checks, on the phone only, whether the maker's battery app is there, so its button can open the right auto-start switch.

These pages

This policy and the terms are published at zilloris.com, on GitHub Pages, a GitHub service. When you open one from Arkiv's Settings, your web browser loads it. Like any web page, that request carries the page's address and your network address, and nothing of yours from Arkiv.

Deleting your data

Uninstalling Arkiv removes everything in its own storage on your phone. Some things stay where you put them until you delete them: backup files, PDFs you saved, pictures saved to your gallery, and calendar dates Arkiv added. To remove the calendar dates, switch off "Also put them in my calendar" before you uninstall; Arkiv asks for calendar access if it needs it. Dates Arkiv could not take out yet, because it had no calendar access, stay if you uninstall first: delete them in your calendar app, where they are titled "Renew a paper — open Arkiv".

Crash reports and usage counts already sent to Firebase are held under a random identifier with no account attached. The detailed records are deleted after 90 days (crash reports) and 2 months (usage counts); speed timings and ML Kit's notes follow Google's own schedule. We cannot trace any of it back to you. Questions about any of it: the contact above.

Children

Arkiv is not made for children under 13. There is no account, and Arkiv never sends us a name, an email or anything from your papers. The technical data described above does go with random identifiers for the app on your phone and with your network address, and privacy law counts these as personal information. We cannot link them to who you are, whatever your age.
